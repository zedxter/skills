The hardest lesson from my failed startup: effort doesn’t prove you’re making progress.

I could stay busy for weeks—building, refining, planning—and still avoid the question that mattered most: does anyone need this enough to pay for it?

Looking back, a few lessons stand out:

• A compliment isn’t a commitment. “I’d use that” means very little until someone gives you their time, money, or trust.

• Building can become a way to postpone uncomfortable conversations. Another feature feels productive. Asking why someone won’t buy gives you information you can act on.

• Persistence needs checkpoints. Set a date, decide what evidence you need, and be willing to change direction when it doesn’t appear.

• Your company’s outcome isn’t a verdict on your worth. Take responsibility for your decisions without turning every mistake into an identity.

I wish I’d learned these things earlier. The failure cost time, money, and confidence, and I won’t pretend every part of it was necessary.

But it changed how I work. I ask harder questions sooner. I look for behavior behind encouraging words. And I’m more willing to stop work that the evidence no longer supports.

If I started again, I’d spend less time trying to look like a company and more time finding out whether I had a business.
